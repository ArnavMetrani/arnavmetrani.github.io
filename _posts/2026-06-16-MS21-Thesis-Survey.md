# Archaeology
## Lithics Archaeology   
**Title**: Identifying patterns of variation in Acheulean Handaxes of the Central Narmada Valley: A 3D Geometric Morphometric Approach   
**Name**: Sophia Iyyu (MS21159)   
**Local Thesis Guide**: Dr Parth R Chauhan   

**Brief explanation on the academic motivation behind the objectives of your thesis:**   
*Stone tools are the earliest and longest-surviving evidence of hominin behaviour, cognition, and cultural traditions. They are particularly important in regions like India, where fossil evidence is relatively limited due to a tropical-monsoonal climate. There are various types of stone tools, like flakes, cores, hammerstones, choppers, cleavers, knives, and handaxes. Among these, the Handaxe is particularly important because it has a well-defined shape and has been used for a long period, spanning approximately 1.7 million years. Archaeologists have long debated why handaxes show both standardisation and variation in form across regions and time periods, and what the causes of this are. In my thesis, I sought to address aspects of this long-standing debate over patterns of shape variation in handaxes using an emerging technique in lithics archaeology known as 3D Geometric Morphometrics. The study region of my research includes three sites in the Central Narmada Valley, located in Madhya Pradesh, which is the only region in India to preserve fossil evidence of an extinct Homo species.*

**Brief explanation of your thesis and its outcomes:**   
*My thesis aims to identify patterns of variation in Handaxes, a type of stone tool. To do this, I make use of a technique known as 3D Geometric Morphometrics. As part of the methodology, handaxes were first digitally scanned to create detailed 3D models, following which certain softwares were used to convert the models into a cloud of digital points, evenly spaced in 3D space and referred to as landmarks. By studying how each landmark point moves relative to every other landmark across the entire dataset, a comprehensive analysis of handaxe shape trends was conducted, and major aspects of shape variation were identified using Principal Component Analysis. This approach enabled the study of subtle differences in form, symmetry, and standardisation while separating shape from overall size. I identified new trends, including changes in bilateral symmetry. The study also helped quantify which sites had more similar handaxes than others and which areas of the handaxe showed this variation. More comprehensive studies, including environmental analyses and experimental work, will be required to understand the causes of the observed patterns and link them to understand early human behaviour and cognition.*

**Why did you decide to choose this topic/problem for your thesis?**    
*My guide, Dr Parth, asked me whether I wanted to work on stone tools or animal fossils when we first met to discuss my thesis topic. I decided to work on stone tools, so he suggested I try 3D Geometric Morphometrics, as it was an interesting and emerging technique in research related to Lithic Archaeology. I specifically chose to work on Handaxes because they are long-debated stone tools characterised by a peculiar teardrop shape and by their widespread presence in the stone tool record. Moreover, they are comparatively easier to study using 3D Geometric Morphometrics because they have well-defined shapes.*

**How did you go about learning and researching for your thesis?**    
*Since I am not from this field I tried to get acquainted with basic things like how to identify different types of stone tools and identify their attributes by working for 2 months with summer interns in the lab who were B.A. Archaeology students. Then I read a lot of papers and books to chart down the methodology of my work and identify the major softwares required to do it. There were 4 major parts to my work and each took different amount of time :-*   
_1. 3D Scanning [1 month]_    
_2. Reading research papers to decide workflow [1 month]_    
_3. Data Processing and Statistical Analysis [4-5 months]_    
_4. Writing the thesis [1 month]_   

**Are there any aspects you wished you spent more/less time on?**  
*No.*

**Do you plan to continue working on your thesis topic in the future, or changing to something else?**    
*I haven't decided this.*

**How can one learn more about work related to your thesis?**   
*You can read research papers on the topic; there is also a book titled 'New Perspective on Old Stones', which is good to understand the use of Geometric Morphometrics on Stone tools. Copies of the book are available in the Paleoarch Lab at IISER Mohali.*

**Title**: Black Hole Hair [(Poster)](https://github.com/ArnavMetrani/arnavmetrani.github.io/blob/f946c0df8bfae79af51a061431523702137e0513/_data/TEMPORARY/ThesisPoster_Black%20Hole%20Hair%20-%20Veera%20Vaishnavi%20Godithi.pdf)

**Local Thesis Guide**: Dr. Kinjalk Lochan

**Brief explanation on the academic motivation behind the objectives of your thesis:**    
*There are only limited Black Hole solutions that we know of and each solution is characterized by a parameter which is a conserved quantity: Mass, Charge and Angular Momentum parameters. Apart from these properties of a Black Hole, we do not know any other physical feature of the black hole. This shows us that we have very limited information about the black hole itself. Finding Black hole hair can help us solve the Hawking Paradox. The notion of (quantum) hair might allow information about what goes into a black hole to come out again without violating any of the important principles of general relativity or quantum mechanics, helping us solve the paradox.*

**Brief explanation of your thesis and its outcomes:**

I tried to find scalar hair, Electromagnetic Hair and Proca hair but they all turned out to be trivial. In the end, I found out that there might exist scalar hair on this black hole if the scalar field is non-minimally coupled to gravity. I will be working further to find out the solutions for this.*

**Why did you decide to choose this topic/problem for your thesis?**  
*I was interested in GR and theoretical physics. This topic was one of the three topics dr. Kinjalk had suggested and I was excited to learn about Black Hole Hair. And also, in the future, I wanted to move into doing research in the field of Quantum Black Holes, Black Hole thermodynamics etc, and I felt like this would be a good start.*

**How did you go about learning and researching for your thesis?**  
*My work was completely theoretical so most of the work I did was to learn from books, study papers and solve equations on paper. After doing some amount of literature review, I tried to use the methods from those papers to find out hair in the general spherically symmetric static black holes.*

**Any aspects you wished you spent more/less time on?**

## Observational Astronomy

**Title**: Galaxy Evolution across the Large-Scale Structures around Abell 851 at z $\sim$ 0.4 [(Poster)](https://github.com/ArnavMetrani/arnavmetrani.github.io/blob/f946c0df8bfae79af51a061431523702137e0513/_data/TEMPORARY/poster_latest%20-%20Kalachaveedu%20Sravya.pptx)      
**Name**: Sravya Kalachaveedu (MS21131)    
**External Thesis Guide**: Dr Yusei Koyama, National Astronomical Observatory of Japan      
**Local Administrator**: Dr Kulinder Pal Singh    

**Brief explanation on the academic motivation behind the objectives of your thesis:**     
*Galaxies experience different effects on their star formation, shape, and evolution depending on their surroundings, and eventually, many stop forming stars altogether. One of the major questions in this field is understanding where and how these environmental effects become important. By studying a galaxy cluster that is still in the process of assembling, we can observe galaxies in many different environments: a dense cluster core, filamentary structures connected to the core, and smaller, less dense galaxy groups surrounding it. Comparing the properties of galaxies across these different regions helps us identify where galaxies change the most, providing some understanding of galaxy evolution as they move through different environments.*

**Brief explanation of your thesis and its outcomes:**    
*This thesis examines one important property of galaxies: their colour, which serves as an indicator of their star-formation activity. Using deep imaging data from the Subaru Hyper Suprime-Cam, I analysed around 31,000 galaxies in and around the still-forming galaxy cluster Abell 851. The study shows that galaxies in denser environments tend to become redder and less active in forming stars. At the same time, their distance from the cluster centre also affects their star formation activity. The greatest changes are seen in dense surrounding regions such as galaxy groups and filaments, suggesting that galaxies begin to change even before entering the cluster core, a process known as pre-processing. Overall, this work describes how galaxies evolve as they move through different environments in the large-scale structure of the Universe.*

**Why did you decide to choose this topic/problem for your thesis?**  
*I chose this topic because I was particularly interested in working with large-scale survey data and studying how patterns in large datasets can reveal the properties and behaviour of astronomical objects. Galaxy evolution especially appealed to me, since it combines observational data analysis with questions about how galaxies change over time. I was also interested in studying transition periods and understanding the physical reasons behind these changes. While reading papers in this area, I came across my supervisor's work on galaxy clusters and the environmental effects on galaxies. After contacting him, he mentioned that new observational data from the same region had recently become available and had not yet been studied, which made it an exciting opportunity for thesis work.*

**How did you go about learning and researching for your thesis?**  
*Mostly reading papers and trying to understand how certain methods were implemented. The workflow was first to reduce and analyse the data once it was in a useful form, then to test the methods I had read about and interpret the results. I would then discuss with my supervisor, and we would decide whether the analysis provided useful information.*

**Do you plan to continue working on your thesis topic in the future, or changing to something else?**  
*I do plan on working on this same topic in the future, since it is still quite interesting to me. I want to cover everything in more detail, if possible. I also want to broaden the work by including data from more wavelengths, studying other clusters at different redshifts (to provide a comprehensive understanding of these properties over cosmic time), and studying additional properties.*

# Economics

**Title**: The Effects of Digital Payments on the Financial Systems in India [(Poster)](https://github.com/ArnavMetrani/arnavmetrani.github.io/blob/f946c0df8bfae79af51a061431523702137e0513/_data/TEMPORARY/poster_final_A0%20-%20Samarth%20Goel.pdf)     
**Name**: Samarth Goel (MS21230)     
**Local Thesis Guide**: Dr. Debdulal Saha   

**Brief explanation on the academic motivation behind the objectives of your thesis:**    
*The rapid rise of digital payments like India's Unified Payments Interface (UPI) suggests that physical cash should be disappearing. However, a major macroeconomic paradox exists: despite explosive digital adoption, physical cash usage in India remains remarkably high. The primary academic motivation for this thesis was to solve this mystery and investigate whether this massive digital transition has fundamentally broken classical economic rules. Solving this puzzle is critical for real-world policy: central banks, the Reserve Bank of India (RBI), must accurately predict how much money the country needs to function. If the RBI relies on legacy forecasting models that assume physical cash is still being used for daily transactions, they risk making massive policy miscalculations that could destabilize the money supply.*

**Brief explanation of your thesis and its outcomes:**    
*This thesis analyzes three decades of Indian macroeconomic data to uncover how the "UPI revolution" actually changed the economy. The research discovered a split reality. On a macro-level (the big picture), the broader economic machine did not break; the overall speed at which money circulates and the way large banks operate remain fundamentally stable. However, on a micro-level (everyday human behavior), everything changed. Because digital transfers are now instant and free, people no longer need to hold liquid cash for daily expenses. Instead, they sweep their extra money into higher-interest savings accounts and use UPI to pay for things directly. The thesis concludes that the massive amount of physical cash remaining in India (the Cash Paradox) is no longer being used for daily buying-it is simply being hoarded as a precautionary safety net against emergencies (like the COVID-19 pandemic). Ultimately, UPI completely modernized how we transact day-to-day without destroying the fundamental laws of the economy.*

**Why did you decide to choose this topic/problem for your thesis?**     
*I chose this topic because I noticed a disconnect between my everyday life and the macroeconomic data. On a personal level, almost everyone I know has completely stopped carrying a physical cash. UPI has made our daily transactions totally frictionless. But when I looked at the RBI's data, Currency in Circulation was still surging. That 'Currency Demand Paradox' completely fascinated me. I wanted to know if this digital revolution had actually broken the classical laws of macroeconomics, or if we were just misunderstanding how people use money in a digital age.*

## Cold Atoms
**Title**: Negative group velocity and pulse distortion by a cold atom ensemble   
**Name**: Arnav Metrani (MS21254)     
**External Thesis Guide**: Dr. Mathilde Hugbart, Institut de Physique de Nice (INPHYNI)   
**Local Administrator**: Dr. Balanarayan   

**Brief explanation on the academic motivation behind the objectives of your thesis:**    
*We aimed to maximize the fractional advance with low distortion for two reasons:*   
*1. Our current experimental setup is exceptionally sensitive, so we can study pulse propagation through an optically dense medium (optical depth $b \gtrsim$ 12) while working in the linear regime, i.e. without being forced to use a power enough laser input such that a signifcant proportion of the atomic population becomes inverted. Negative group velocity in cold atoms relies on the linear regime and is amplified by increasing optical depth. Hence, running these tests would also serve as an effective way of testing the sensitivty of the experiment.   
2. Owing to the sensitivity of the experimental setup, my lab group researchers hypothesized that we should be able to achieve a better fractional advance in cold atoms than the current literature. In addition, this would've been a significant improvement in the domain of low-distortion outputs. Hence, the thesis topic.*

**Brief explanation of your thesis and its outcomes:**   
*The group velocity of a pulse is the velocity ascribed to the envelope center of a travelling light pulse. For a Gaussian pulse (as well as Gaussian-like pulses, ie. when the amplitude increases and decreases smoothly), we define the group velocity as the velocity of the pulse's peak intensity. All good so far. In the 20th century it was theorized that there exist cases where a light pulse is said to have propagated through an optical medium with a "negative" group velocity, which implied that the pulse peak exited the medium even before the initial pulse peak had entered. After intense delibration, it was shown rigourously in the late 20th century that there is no violation of causality.*   
 *However there was still the remaining question of the experimentally maximising this effect. The longer the duration of a (Gaussian) pulse, the higher the time advance of the output peak from the medium versus its propagation in vacuum over the same length. However, the fractional advance (time advance/total duration) decreases. If we try to maximize this fraction, then the experimental parameters that we set end up distorting the pulse.*    
 *The goal of the thesis was based on this: What is the maximum fractional advance for low distortion that we can achieve in our cold atoms experimental setup, and is it better than the literature?*
 *In my thesis, I analysed the propagation of light through a disordered atom cloud via theoretical and computational methods, quantified the distortion via different measures, and identified the optimal experimental parameters for maximising this effect. I was able to show that we should expect to perform considerably better than previous results in atomic media in the case of low-distortion. We also observe that the conventional group velocity deviates strongly from the simulated group velocity. The deviation occurs even at moderate fractional advance and holds irrespective of distortion. Unfortuantely we were not able to obtain the experimental results by the completion of my thesis, however I performed characterization of some of the optical components (acousto-optic-modulator and electro-optic-modulator) and determined the most suitable method for generating the desired light pulses.*

**Why did you decide to choose this topic/problem for your thesis?**     
*This was not my initial thesis topic; my initial topic was much more experimental in nature and completely different, however there were some issues with the experiment which severely delayed it. As a result, I was given this topic to work on. Fortunately I came to like the topic quite intensely: it is an extremely counterintuitive topic, I get to learn and work on something completely new to me, and I had/have a lot "naive" questions which gave me a lot to think about.*   
*I was mainly interested in working on quantum optics and optical traps as I've always found them fascinating; such a complicated apparatus to work at the most textbook-like physics conditions.*

**How did you go about learning and researching for your thesis?**    
*With a lot of time spent headracking on very simple points. I spent a lot of time on the initial chapters of Boyd's Nonlinear Optics, reading and following the calculations carried out in reference papers, performing a lot of heuristic 'bad' calculations so that atleast I have some ballpark of what I should expect. I think this helped me since I needed some way to figure out whether the corresponding simulation code that I'm writing is complete gibberish or not, especially since I had no suitable reference to directly cross-check with.   
Other than this, especially on the experimental side I spent a lot of time reading the instruction manuals to figure out how can I use the equipment effectively. Lot of time spent tinkering with parameters and aligning the pesky optical components.   
Lastly, speaking to other PhDs, postdoc, my supervisor about simple doubts helped clarify a lot. StackExchange as well.*

**Any aspects you wished you spent more/less time on?**   
*Some planning would've gone a very long way. I thought that 6 months in the lab is a world of time, so I spent a lot of it delibrating on points that although educational, simply weren't the most efficient way to achieve the project's objectives. I spent 3 weeks on a training apparatus and a lot of time on loosely-related theory. So I would say that a) you won't have enough time for your thesis if you plan on doing absolutely everything b) you will have enough time once you're done with your thesis to go back to all the things you wanted to spend a lot of time earlier. Spending more time on the experimental setup would've been helpful since you can always go back to your books in your off-time. Hindsight is 20-20.* 

**Do you plan to continue working on your thesis topic in the future, or changing to something else?**     
*I want to continue working on the topic in the short term. The experiment is yet to be run. There are a lot of small points that I am interested in exploring myself. For instance, looking at other frequency spectrums, trying to find pseudo-closed expressions for the group velocity and fractional advance, looking at its applications in interferometers, etc.    
I would not be extremely interested in pursuing it further there are some concrete ways in which the work can be extended. Furthermore, the theory section of it (in the semi-classical setting and for lossy media) is completely settled, and there aren't any atomic physics labs that are working on this topic. However I am looking towards working in atomic physics or quantum optics, as the setups are so versatile that there isn't a lot that you cannot explore with them.*

Milonni titled "[Fast Light, Slow Light and Left-Handed Light](https://doi.org/10.1201/9780367801557)" which builds up the background and also goes into the full-QM side. ## High Energy Physics   
**Title**: Test of Quantum Entanglement in the $\Upsilon (4S) \to B^{0} \bar B^{0}$ System at Belle II via $B^0 \to D^{*-} \ell^{+} \nu_{\ell}$ Decays. [(Poster)](https://github.com/ArnavMetrani/arnavmetrani.github.io/blob/f946c0df8bfae79af51a061431523702137e0513/_data/TEMPORARY/ms_poster_tejas%20-%20Tejas%20Rana.pdf)  
**Name**: Tejas Rana (MS21024)     
**External Thesis Guide**: Dr. Hans Günther Moser, Max Planck Institute for Physics, Munich   
**Local Administrator**: Dr. Vishal Bhardwaj   

**Brief explanation on the academic motivation behind the objectives of your thesis:**    
*One of the biggest unsolved questions in physics is why the universe has more matter than antimatter. If the Big Bang produced both in equal amounts, they should have annihilated each other completely, leaving nothing behind. Yet here we are. Something broke that symmetry early on, and understanding what exactly is a central goal of modern particle physics. A key part of this is studying CP violation, a subtle asymmetry between matter and antimatter in how certain particles decay. Precision measurements of CP violation rely on an important assumption: that certain particle pairs produced at colliders are quantum entangled. Two particles born together share a quantum state, meaning neither has a definite identity until one of them decays. This kind of correlation has been confirmed in photon experiments many times, but it is much harder to verify in heavier, short-lived particles like B mesons. Some theoretical proposals suggest this entanglement might not always be perfectly preserved. A small fraction of B meson pairs could lose their quantum correlations right at production and behave as independent classical particles instead. If that happens even at the $10%$ level, it would silently bias CP violation measurements without anyone noticing. The existing experimental constraints are not tight enough to rule that out. That is what motivates this work.*

**Brief explanation of your thesis and its outcomes:**   
*B mesons are heavy, short-lived particles produced in pairs at the Belle II experiment in Japan. They are born from the decay of an extremely short-lived particle called the $\Upsilon(4S)$, and they come out in a quantum entangled state, meaning neither has a definite identity until one of them decays. My thesis measures how well this entanglement is actually preserved, using a parameter called $\zeta$. If $\zeta$ is zero, quantum mechanics holds exactly. If it is non-zero, some fraction of pairs have already lost their entanglement at the moment of production. To measure $\zeta$, I reconstruct one of the two B mesons through a specific decay chain, $B^0\to D^{*-}\ell^+\nu_\ell$ where a B meson decays into a $D^*$ meson, a lepton, and a neutrino. The lepton charge directly identifies which type of B meson decayed, which is important for the measurement. By looking at the time difference between the two B decays across many events, a characteristic pattern emerges in the data that is sensitive to $\zeta$. Fitting this pattern gives an estimate of the parameter. The full analysis is carried out on simulated data, covering everything from reconstructing decay products, estimating backgrounds, to the final fit for $\zeta$. Processing tens of millions of simulated events, the reconstruction pipeline is the primary technical contribution of this thesis. The workflow is designed to be directly applied to real Belle II data in the future, where a precise measurement of $\zeta$ would either confirm that quantum entanglement is fully maintained in B meson pairs, or reveal a small but physically important deviation from standard quantum mechanics.*

**Why did you decide to choose this topic/problem for your thesis?**     
*The path was actually very simple. I have always loved understanding nature at its most fundamental level, where the rules governing everything are written. That is the main reason I chose particle physics, even though I had no prior experience in HEP before starting my master's. I love theory with all my heart, but I also like seeing real results from my work. That is what drew me to the experimental side. I was not picky about any particular experiment either, CERN, Belle II, DUNE, anything would have made me happy. I just wanted a genuine experience in this field. After a long search, I fortunately found my way to the Belle II group at the Max-Planck-Institut für Physik in Munich. The problem of quantum entanglement in B meson pairs was just a cherry on top of this curiosity.*

**How did you go about learning and researching for your thesis?**     
*My work was highly computational. I personally like to understand the theory and the basic framework of whatever I am working with before jumping into the application or coding part. But I have observed a major pitfall in this approach. Because I like to understand things at the most intuitive level, I would go too deep into the theory, so deep that after a week or two I would realise I had gone completely off on a tangent from what I was actually supposed to be doing. So for anyone reading this who works the same way: if your end goal is on the application side, set a personal deadline for the theory part. Skim over it first, get a basic feel for what is going on, then start coding. Keep going back to the theory whenever you feel stuck. That way you are coding with context and intention, rather than coding in the dark after months of reading. I think a modified version of this applies to theoretical work as well.*

**Any aspects you wished you spent more/less time on?**   
*Honestly, the theory. I always wish I had spent more time on it. But knowing myself, if I had given it free rein, I would have spent all nine months just reading and never actually done the analysis. So in that sense, given the time I had, I think I struck a reasonable balance and did the best I could.*

**Do you plan to continue working on your thesis topic in the future, or changing to something else?**     
*Yes, I am continuing this project. Most of the work on simulated Monte Carlo data is done. What remains is fixing the $\Delta t$ fit, estimating the systematic uncertainty, and then getting the analysis approved by the Belle II collaboration before applying the full pipeline to real data. I plan to keep working on this at least until my PhD starts.*

**How can one learn more about work related to your thesis?**     
*For anyone thinking of working on a Belle II project, or any HEP project for the first time, I would highly recommend starting with Bachelor, Master, or PhD theses on topics as close to yours as possible. After that, find the publications closest to your topic and look for the detailed internal notes of your experiment associated with them, whether that is a Belle II note, an ATLAS note, a CMS note, or whatever collaboration you are working with. Read only the sections relevant to what you are working on at that moment, otherwise everything can feel overwhelming very quickly. Even better if all these theses and publications are from your own working group. But even then, you can easily spend a lot of time on things that are not directly useful to you right now. So above all these resources, my honest advice is to simply ask. Ask your supervisor, postdocs, PhD students, master's students, anyone who knows more than you do. It might feel like a big inconvenience to disturb others, but everybody understands that all these things can feel overwhelming, you are not supposed to know everything, and most people are genuinely happy to help. You will save yourself an enormous amount of time.*

**Name**: Jyoti Prakash Behera (MS21003)     
**Local Thesis Guide**: Dr. Satyajit Jena   

**Brief explanation of your thesis and its outcomes:**   
*The aim of this thesis is to study Linear Accelerators (Linacs) on the context of their applications in external beam radiotherapy (EBRT). Especially, EBRT based on hadrons such as protons and carbon-ions is focused on. A major challenge is real time verification of beam dose deposition. Conventional imaging techniques lack temporal resolution. To address this, we use prompt gammas, which are emitted instantaneously during nuclear interactions between the incident proton beam and target nuclei, providing real-time information on beam penetration and interaction depth. At the same time, equipped with a dependable imaging system, we investigate the quality of the beam using several beam parameters. We study beam dynamics and beam transport in Linacs to do this. By combining imaging and beam optimization, we would have robust, real-time clinically viable proton therapy system..*
